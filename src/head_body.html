Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology

You've been told you need a thyroid FNAC. Here's what that means.

A fine needle aspiration takes a few cells from a lump in your thyroid, so a pathologist can look at them under a microscope and work out what the lump is. This page explains what it is, what happens on the day, what you'll feel, and what comes after.

1 What a thyroid nodule actually is

A thyroid nodule is an abnormal growth of thyroid cells into a lump inside your thyroid. The thyroid is a gland at the front of your neck, below your voice box, that makes hormones controlling how your body uses energy.

Most thyroid nodules are benign — that means non-cancerous. But some can be cancerous, and no one can tell just by looking at your neck. That is why your doctor has asked for this test.

Thyroid nodules are common, and most are benign. Most people with one are simply monitored, but a small number need further tests or treatment — that is what the FNAC is checking.

2 Why you need this test

The purpose of the FNAC is to find out the nature of the nodule and to try to rule out the possibility of cancer in your thyroid gland.

FNAC is the standard first test for this. It gives your doctor real information — enough to decide whether you need treatment, further tests, or simply to be monitored.

4 What you'll feel

This is what people worry about most, so here it is honestly:

  • Pressure on your neck from the ultrasound probe resting there.
  • A slight pin prick from the needle. It is finer than the needle used for a routine blood test.
  • Some pressure as the samples are taken.
  • You may feel very little. Most people describe it as quick, and less uncomfortable than they expected.

You'll be asked to stay as still as you can and to avoid coughing, talking or swallowing while the needle is in, because those movements shift the thyroid and make accurate placement harder.

5 How it's done

The procedure is done by inserting a fine needle into the nodule and making several passes to obtain a small sample of tissue, which is sent to the laboratory for evaluation.

It is carried out under ultrasound guidance so the needle is placed accurately — using the same scanning that found the nodule.

You will usually be offered a local anaesthetic injection to numb the site. It is optional, but it is commonly given.

6 Before the procedure

Usually no preparation is needed.

Tell your doctor if you are on any blood thinners or antiplatelets — medicines such as warfarin, rivaroxaban, aspirin or clopidogrel. Tell them before the procedure, and do not stop any of these on your own. Your doctor will say whether any should be paused, and when to restart them.

Tell your doctor about any blood thinners or antiplatelet medicines you take.
You do not normally need to stop eating or drinking beforehand.
Wear something comfortable that lets your neck be reached easily.
Write down your questions — nothing is too small to ask.

7 Risks and complications

FNAC is generally safe. Minor effects such as slight pain, bruising or swelling are common but usually settle quickly; serious complications are rare. These are the risks your doctor wants you to know about:

Up to about 20% of FNAC results may return as non-diagnostic or inadequate, because not enough cells were sampled for the pathologist to make a clear diagnosis. If this happens, the usual next step is to repeat the test — another small sample is taken — and your doctor will discuss whether any further tests are needed.

9 Getting your results

The results usually take about 2 weeks to process. Your primary doctor will arrange to tell you the results.

It is important to attend the follow-up appointment so the results can be discussed properly with you. Waiting can feel long — that is normal, and the wait itself does not mean anything has gone wrong.

10 What if you'd rather not have it?

If you are not keen for FNAC, your doctor will not be able to determine the nature of the nodule and to rule out the possibility of cancer within the thyroid gland. You should discuss alternative options for managing your thyroid nodule with your doctor, e.g. monitoring the nodule with regular ultrasound or excision biopsy of the nodule for histology. These options would be best discussed with your primary (referring) doctor.
There is a chance that the nodule will turn out to be cancerous and you risk missing the necessary treatment.

If anything here is unclear or worrying, ask your doctor. Bring this page with you — it is easier to talk it through together.
